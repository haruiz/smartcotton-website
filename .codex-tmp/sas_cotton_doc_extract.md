Project: Climate-Smart Cotton: Developing Precision Regenerative Practices and Market Opportunities for Addressing Climate Change in the Cotton Belt

Award Number: 2024-68012-41750

The specific objectives of this project are:

Develop regenerative cotton production practices and investigate their long-term effects on US Belt-wide cotton production systems.

Establish soil organic carbon and carbon intensity baselines for ecoregions in the US Cotton Belt;

Investigate regenerative practices for reducing tillage and improving soil health;

Investigate the long-term impact of regenerative practices in addressing climate change using simulation models

Develop and/or evaluate precision AI/ML and smart technologies for resource conservation and climate change mitigation/adaptation in cotton

Evaluate the economic feasibility of precision regenerative production practices and determine new market opportunities

Expand knowledge about farmers’ multi-dimensional experiences of adoption of regenerative practices across the Cotton Belt

Promote the adoption of regenerative, climate-smart cotton production practices through innovative and collaborative extension and outreach activities

Provide educational opportunities to train the next generation of research and extension scientists and practitioners, including underserved communities, and empower the rural workforce

What was accomplished under these goals? (2025-26)

During the reporting period, the project made strong progress across multi-state field research, soil and microbial analyses, regenerative practice evaluation, precision technology development, economic analysis, and farmer adoption studies. Activities were implemented across Texas, New Mexico, Arizona, Georgia, North Carolina, Mississippi, Alabama, and Tennessee, generating important datasets and strengthening the foundation for future research, outreach, and training.

Objective 1. Develop regenerative production practices and determine their long-term effects on US cotton.

During this reporting period, the project team made strong progress in evaluating regenerative cotton production practices across multiple states. In Texas, Dr. Jake Mowrer’s team collected soil samples from cotton sites under five tillage and cover crop treatments. Soil organic carbon, respiration, and wet aggregate stability measurements were completed, with AMF and phosphatase analyses underway. Dr. Katie Lewis completed the first cotton/forage sorghum rotation cycle and assisted with soil sampling from 20–30 High Plains farms. Dr. Nithya Rajan’s team evaluated bed configuration and cover crop termination timing in Texas Blackland Vertisols, with preliminary results showing that cover crops maintained or improved yield performance, especially under the two-week termination treatment.

In New Mexico, Dr. Rajan Ghimire’s team completed first-year soil health data collection and analysis for the circular grass buffer strips project, collected soil profile carbon and nitrogen samples, identified on-farm sampling sites, presented preliminary results, and had one manuscript accepted. In Arizona, Dr. Debankur Sanyal demonstrated cover crop production on beds in rotation with upland cotton under irrigated conditions, with cover crop treatments producing higher lint yield than controls. In Georgia, Dr. Nicholas Basinger completed SOC and bulk density sampling from 20 fields and AMF sampling from 10 fields. In North Carolina, Dr. Ramon Leon-Gonzalez collected soil and mid-season AMF samples from five cotton fields, with additional sampling planned from 25 fields. Microbiome and root-trait work also advanced. Dr. Sanjay Antony-Babu’s team completed DNA extraction and 16S sequencing for samples from Brazos Bottom and Lamesa, showing distinct microbial signatures between different production systems. Auburn teams evaluated cotton lines and cultivars for weed suppression, photosynthetic traits, and root traits. Mississippi State completed all 30 on-farm soil sampling sites and collected key seasonal crop, soil, and cover crop measurements. Agricenter International implemented field treatments, including flame weeding, mulch, cultivation, and autonomous tractor operations, with data collected on labor, weed control, plant response, and yield.

Objective 2. Develop and/or evaluate precision AI/ML and smart technologies for resource conservation and climate change adaptation in cotton.

Progress focused on building AI/ML-ready datasets and advancing simulation tools for climate-smart cotton production. Dr. Mahendra Bhandari and Dr. Uday Vaddevolu recruited Sabin Shrestha, who began his Ph.D. program in Spring 2026. The team also developed a comprehensive data acquisition plan with research programs, institutes, and industry partners involved in irrigation research. This plan will integrate data from soil moisture sensors, UAS platforms, satellite imagery, and agronomic measurements, including plant height, biomass, yield, fiber quality, irrigation amounts, and treatment strategies. These multi-source datasets will help assess existing data resources and identify additional experimental needs for training and validating AI/ML models across cotton-growing regions.

GaiaDhi AgTech, in collaboration with Texas A&M, advanced simulation-based evaluation of regenerative practices for climate change adaptation. The Holos DSS model was configured and calibrated for the TAMU cotton test farm, with validation testing completed for yield. The team extracted modeled soil CO₂ emission trends across farming practices, simulated planting date effects on cotton yield, and initiated crop rotation simulations to evaluate climate-friendly and adaptive cotton production strategies.

Objective 3. Evaluate the economic feasibility of precision regenerative production practices and determine new market opportunities.

Progress focused on evaluating the economic feasibility, profitability, risk, and market potential of precision regenerative cotton practices. Dr. Darren Hudson’s team completed a meta-analysis of U.S.-based agronomic studies on precision application and soil health practices, including guidance systems, variable rate application, cover crops, and pasture management. For cotton, guidance systems and variable rate planters were identified as important practices for improving yield and profitability. The team also analyzed risk and insurance impacts of soil health improvements using Monte Carlo simulation and Texas High Plains data. Results showed that improved soil health can increase yield and reduce crop failure risk in dryland cotton, with yield benefits outweighing higher insurance costs.

Objective 4. Expand knowledge about farmers’ multi-dimensional experiences of adoption of regenerative practices across the Cotton Belt.

Progress focused on understanding producer adoption of regenerative practices and the social dimensions of soil health management. Dr. Jennifer Jo Thompson’s team facilitated three in-person focus groups in South Georgia with nine current or prospective cotton farmers, in collaboration with the Soil Health Institute, to examine adoption challenges related to cover crops and other regenerative practices. Results were analyzed and shared through an internal report, social media post, UGA Impact Statement, and a presentation at the 2025 American Anthropological Association meeting, with a manuscript in progress. The team also supported learning through the 2026 Southern Cover Crop Council Conference, farm visits, and farmer discussions. A field survey instrument was developed with UGA and SHI collaborators for Georgia producers participating in SAS CAP soil sampling, with plans to share it across other Cotton Belt teams after piloting.

Objective 5. Promote the adoption of regenerative, climate-smart cotton production practices through innovative and collaborative extension and outreach activities.

Extension and outreach activities are planned for the later project years, with Dr. Benjamin McKnight’s component scheduled for 2028–2029. Dr. Peter Dotray shared SAS Smart Cotton research updates through county meetings, a public radio segment, agricultural conferences, and university symposiums, reaching farmers, consultants, industry, students, and stakeholders. Four undergraduate students also gained hands-on training in field and greenhouse weed science activities.

Objective 6. Provide educational opportunities to train the next generation of research and extension scientists and practitioners, including underserved communities, and empower the rural workforce.

The project also contributed to student training and workforce development. Four undergraduate students from Texas Tech University were employed and supported through the project and gained hands-on experience in field and greenhouse research related to chemical and non-chemical weed control tactics in agronomic cropping systems. These students were trained in weed identification, sprayer calibration, small plot data collection, data entry, and exposure to various farming practices. More broadly, project activities across locations continued to provide practical research experience and professional development opportunities for students and trainees involved in regenerative and climate-smart cotton research.
