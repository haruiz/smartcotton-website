[Normal] What are the major goals of this project?
[Normal] The long-term goal of the proposed research is to apply improved precision management practices to enable enhanced carbon sequestration and reduced greenhouse gas emission, pest control, and nutrient and water management; and to address labor challenges, steward land, create new market opportunities, and ensure a sustainable supply of climate-smart cotton.
[Normal] The specific objectives of this project are:
[List Paragraph] Develop regenerative cotton production practices and investigate their long-term effects on US Belt-wide cotton production systems.
[List Paragraph] Establish soil organic carbon and carbon intensity baselines for ecoregions in the US Cotton Belt;
[List Paragraph] Investigate regenerative practices for reducing tillage and improving soil health;
[List Paragraph] Investigate the long-term impact of regenerative practices in addressing climate change using simulation models
[List Paragraph] Develop and/or evaluate precision AI/ML and smart technologies for resource conservation and climate change mitigation/adaptation in cotton
[List Paragraph] Evaluate the economic feasibility of precision regenerative production practices and determine new market opportunities
[List Paragraph] Expand knowledge about farmers’ multi-dimensional experiences of adoption of regenerative practices across the Cotton Belt
[List Paragraph] Promote the adoption of regenerative, climate-smart cotton production practices through innovative and collaborative extension and outreach activities
[List Paragraph] Provide educational opportunities to train the next generation of research and extension scientists and practitioners, including underserved communities, and empower the rural workforce

[Normal] Annual Report Summary (2024-25): Success Stories
[Normal] Sanjay Antony-Babu
[Normal] Success: Design and Implementation of a Novel Hyphal-Baiting Technique
[Normal] Method: Field soil was added to mason jars and autoclaved thrice. Glycerol stocks of soil slurry (either from bulk or rhizosphere soil) were washed and pipetted on top of the jarred soil. Pre-germinated upland cotton seeds were planted 12 hours later.
[Normal] Procedure: After 3 days, small holes were dug near the base of the seedlings, into which a 100µm-mesh cell strainer with an agar plug of Fov1 was placed. The plugs were allowed to grow into the soil for 4 days before removal. The soil adhering to the strainers, often with visible hyphal overgrowth, was removed and plated on 0.5xPDA plates to grow for 7 days.
[Normal] Outcome: Hyphal tips were scraped and added to molecular-grade water for DNA extraction and sequencing.

Throughout the project’s lifecycle, various activities, events, and products are expected to lead to impactful outcomes in the areas of field research, stakeholder engagement, and student training. [List Paragraph] Activities
[Normal] Field, Greenhouse, and Laboratory Experiments The project team is conducting a variety of field trials, greenhouse studies, and laboratory experiments to explore sustainable cotton production practices. These studies aim to assess the impact of regenerative approaches, such as the integration of cover crops, soil health management, and precision irrigation practices, on cotton yield and resilience to climate-related stressors. For instance:
[Normal] Reyes et al. studied how cover crops affect root characteristics and photosynthetic traits in Alabama cotton fields.
[Normal] Research by Sapkota et al. focused on mitigating greenhouse gas emissions through grass buffer strips in irrigation circles.
[Normal] Laboratory research by Elek Nagy involved developing a novel method to bait hyphosphere bacteria from soil samples, which could improve our understanding of microbial interactions in cotton systems.

[Normal (Web)] This integrated SAS Climate-Smart Cotton project is expected to generate substantial benefits for the U.S. cotton industry, particularly through the development and implementation of regenerative practices aimed at reducing tillage and addressing the challenges posed by climate change. Over the duration of the project, the anticipated outcomes will span environmental, economic, and social impacts. By leveraging cutting-edge precision agriculture technologies, this project will create new market opportunities, enhance sustainability, and improve farm profitability. The key expected outcomes include:
[Normal (Web)] Environmental and Sustainability Outcomes
[Normal (Web)] Reduction in Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Emissions One of the primary environmental outcomes of this project is the significant reduction in greenhouse gas emissions across cotton production systems. Through the integration of regenerative practices such as reduced tillage, cover cropping, and soil health improvement techniques, the project aims to enhance carbon sequestration in soils, effectively mitigating GHG emissions. Research conducted on grass buffer strips and other conservation practices is expected to provide concrete evidence of how these methods contribute to emission reductions in water-limited agroecosystems.
[Normal (Web)] Improved Soil Health A critical focus of this project is enhancing soil health by incorporating sustainable practices like reduced tillage and the use of cover crops. These practices help in maintaining and increasing organic matter, improving soil structure, enhancing microbial diversity, and reducing soil erosion. As a result, cotton growers will experience improved soil fertility, leading to better crop yields over time, even in regions that may face water scarcity or other climate-related stressors.
[Normal (Web)] Increased Water Use Efficiency The project will contribute to the improved management of water resources through precision irrigation technologies and smart-water management practices. By integrating real-time data from soil moisture sensors and weather forecasts, cotton producers will be able to better manage irrigation schedules, reducing water waste and ensuring that crops receive the necessary water for optimal growth. This outcome will also help farmers adapt to the challenges of drought and water scarcity, which are becoming more prevalent due to climate change.
[Normal (Web)] Promotion of Climate-Resilient Cotton Production The project’s regenerative practices will ultimately lead to a more resilient cotton production system. By improving soil health, water use efficiency, and pest management, the project will help farmers adapt to extreme weather conditions, such as droughts and heavy rainfall, which are becoming more frequent due to climate change. Additionally, the implementation of precision agriculture technologies will enable early diagnosis of field issues, allowing for more proactive responses to weather-related disruptions.
[Normal (Web)] Economic Outcomes
[Normal (Web)] Increased Input Use Efficiency By utilizing precision agriculture technologies, this project will enable cotton producers to diagnose field issues promptly and implement site-specific interventions that improve input use efficiency. These technologies, including soil moisture sensors, automated irrigation systems, and data-driven decision-making tools, will reduce the overuse of fertilizers, water, and pesticides, lowering production costs for farmers. These improvements in resource efficiency will contribute directly to increasing farm profitability, especially for small and medium-sized operations.
[Normal (Web)] Market Opportunities for Climate-Smart Cotton A major outcome of this project is the creation of new market opportunities for cotton producers through the promotion of climate-smart practices. As consumer demand for sustainably produced products rises, cotton grown using regenerative practices such as reduced tillage and cover cropping will be in high demand. The project will support the development of these new market opportunities by providing evidence-based data on the economic benefits of sustainable cotton production. This can open doors to premium markets that focus on environmental sustainability, giving farmers access to higher-value products and more lucrative market niches.
[Normal (Web)] Economic Benefits of Adoption Economic analyses conducted within the project will highlight the tangible economic benefits of adopting precision regenerative practices in cotton production. These analyses will include cost-benefit assessments of different regenerative practices, helping farmers understand the potential for long-term financial gains. Additionally, the economic models developed in the project will quantify the savings in input costs, the value of improved crop yields, and the long-term benefits of sustainability for the cotton industry.
[Normal (Web)] Improved Farm Profitability By promoting cost-saving technologies, improved input use efficiency, and the adoption of precision agriculture practices, this project will directly contribute to increased profitability for cotton farmers. Through reduced input costs and enhanced yields, farmers will experience improved financial stability and competitiveness in both domestic and global markets. Furthermore, the increased use of automation and smart technologies will allow for more efficient management of farming operations, further enhancing farm profitability.
[Normal (Web)] Social and Workforce Development Outcomes
[Normal (Web)] Improved Health and Safety of Farm Workers Through the adoption of regenerative practices and precision technologies, the project will have positive implications for the health and safety of farm workers. Reduced pesticide use, improved soil health, and more efficient irrigation systems will lead to healthier working environments, reducing exposure to harmful chemicals and improving overall worker well-being. Furthermore, precision agriculture technologies will automate tasks like irrigation management, reducing the need for manual labor in harsh or dangerous conditions, thus enhancing worker safety.
[Normal (Web)] Labor Force Transformation and Opportunities Automation and the integration of new technologies will not only improve productivity but will also create new employment opportunities in rural areas. As cotton farmers adopt smart technologies, there will be an increasing demand for skilled workers to operate and maintain these technologies. The project will help train a new generation of workers skilled in operating precision agriculture tools, fostering new career opportunities in rural communities. This will also help address the ongoing labor shortages in agriculture, particularly in regions where cotton is a primary crop.
[Normal (Web)] Training and Education for Students and Researchers A key outcome of this project will be the training and development of undergraduate and graduate students, as well as postdoctoral researchers, who will gain hands-on experience in regenerative agriculture and precision farming techniques. The project’s research and field activities provide educational opportunities for students in agronomy, plant science, and related fields. Through coursework, field research, and industry engagements, these students will be better prepared for careers in sustainable agriculture and climate-smart technologies.
[Normal (Web)] Strengthened Extension and Outreach Programs The project will also strengthen extension services and outreach programs, enabling farmers and agricultural professionals to adopt innovative and sustainable farming practices. The project’s extensive outreach activities, such as conferences, field days, workshops, and online content, will ensure that climate-smart practices are communicated effectively to a broad audience. Through these outreach efforts, the project will increase the adoption of climate-smart cotton practices and foster a deeper understanding of their benefits among cotton producers and industry stakeholders.
[Normal (Web)] Technological Advancements
[Normal (Web)] Development of Precision Agriculture Tools The integration of precision agriculture technologies, including automated irrigation systems, soil moisture sensors, and data-driven decision-making tools, will be one of the most significant technological advancements resulting from this project. These tools will enable cotton producers to implement site-specific measures that optimize resource use, reduce environmental impacts, and improve crop yields. The widespread adoption of these technologies will modernize cotton farming practices and set a new standard for sustainability in the industry.
[Normal (Web)] Automation and Smart Technologies Automation will play a critical role in transforming cotton production practices. [Normal] Objective 1. Develop regenerative cotton production practices and investigate their long-term effects on US Belt-wide cotton production systems. Objective 1.1. In this project, a ‘baseline scenario’ or ‘business as usual’ site will be a cotton field where management for the past 5 years is considered typical for the region with minimal or no use of conservation management practices. Objective 1. 2. Investigate regenerative practices for reducing tillage and improving soil health. 1.2.1. Evaluate locally suitable regenerative production practices focusing on cover crops and living mulches in combination with conservation tillage on weed suppression, soil & water conservation, nutrient cycling, and pest dynamics [Years 1 to 4]: This objective includes four common experiments (CE 1 to CE4) to be conducted across multiple ecoregions to generate necessary research knowledge for promoting the adoption of regenerative practices. The CE1 will evaluate tillage, cover crop, and living mulch systems in controlled on-station field experiments across six ecoregions. The CE2 (3 sites) will evaluate cover crop planting method for cotton grown on beds in high-rainfall areas. The CE3 (4 sites) will focus on determining locally-suitable living mulch species for cotton across the six sites, whereas the CE4 (4 sites) will evaluate living mulch sensitivity to cotton herbicides for sound integration into existing weed management programs. 1.2.2. Track outcomes of reduced tillage and other regenerative production practices that create quantifiable changes in soil health, soil carbon, and GHG emissions [Years 1 to 5]: Soil samples (from Obj 1.1 and CE1) will be analyzed for various soil health indicators: soil pH (Thomas 1996), soil EC (Rhoades 1996), cation exchange capacity (Sikora and Moore 2014), extractable phosphorus (Olsen and Sommers 1982; Sikora and Moore 2014), phosphatase enzyme activity (Tabatabai 1994), extractable potassium, total nitrogen (dry combustion using an Elementar vario MAX Cube), wet aggregate stability (Fajardo et al. 2016), dry bulk density, soil organic carbon (dry combustion after pretreatment to remove inorganic carbon using an Elementar vario MAX Cube), soil respiration (laboratory incubation), and microbiome composition. 1.2.3. Investigate the impact of reduced tillage and regenerative production practices on soil microbiome and soil-borne pathogens in cotton [Years 1 to 4]: Alpha diversity will be obtained using amplicon sequence variant (ASV) method on QIIME2, and taxonomy curated with RDP and SILVA. Beta diversity will be assessed using Bray-Curtis and UNIFRAC distances, Co-occurrence networks will be built to identify keystone bacterial taxa in different samples (Berry and Widder 2014). 1.2.4. Evaluate circular buffer strips of native perennial grasses in center pivot irrigated cotton fields to improve soil health and ecosystem services [Years 1 to 4]: We are proposing a large landscape-level trial at Clovis, NM. A perennial grass mixture of two cool-season grasses and five warm-season grasses was planted in buffer strips in 2016 summer as part of a corn study that is still in place. 1.2.5. Evaluate and determine root traits of cotton for sustainability under climate change [Years 1 to 4]: Screen for cotton cultivars with root characteristics that can increase nutrient (N, P) and water uptake, C sequestration, and weed suppression. Images will be taken and analyzed for root angles, numbers, complexity, and density using the Rhizovision software (Seethepalli et al. 2020; Singh et al. 2021). Objective 1.3. Investigate the long-term impact of regenerative practices in addressing climate change using simulation models [Years 1 to 4]: We will use the widely used process-based cotton simulation modeling platform CSM-CROPGRO-Cotton model in DSSAT.

[Normal] The sustainability of US cotton production is pivotal to agricultural sustainability in the Southern US. Protecting and improving soil health, reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, and enhancing input use efficiency are central to improving the sustainability of cotton in the face of changing climatic conditions. According to the most recent USDA-ERS report on tillage and conservation cropping in the US (Claassen et al. 2018), 60% of cotton acreage is still under conventional tillage, compared for example, to only 30% or less in soybean. Tillage intensity has increased due to the need to control herbicide-resistant and other problematic weeds (CAST 2012), which is now widespread throughout the US Cotton Belt (Heap 2023). Intensive tillage contributes greatly to greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, stored carbon loss, and a loss of overall soil health, while conservation tillage is considered a vital component of a sustainable soil health management system (Elder and Lal 2008; USDA-NRCS 2017). Cotton is inherently a low residue crop, which in turn adds limited amounts of carbon during the growing season. Because cotton is commonly grown with low residue crops in rotation (Claassen et al. 2018), this is a major area where rapid gains can be achieved in soil health improvement and climate change mitigation. In this regard, cover crops and living mulches can play a key role (Wick et al. 2017). A recent analysis by the Cotton 2040 initiative revealed that cotton is a vulnerable crop to climate change impacts (WTW 2022). There is a crucial need to make cotton cultivation a carbon-neutral, or better yet a net carbon-positive enterprise, while simultaneously mitigating/adapting to the adverse effects of climate change. The majority of US cotton is already grown in marginal soils with low organic matter content. Potential increases in tillage to manage the anticipated surge in herbicide-resistant weeds associated with climate change (Ramesh et al. 2017) is expected to exacerbate this problem. Further, the use of Artificial Intelligence/Machine learning (AI/ML)-assisted precision application technologies can greatly improve resource input use efficiency, which can in turn reduce GHG emissions (e.g. Cotton Inc. 2018). These precision regenerative practices are also expected to create new market opportunities, through, for example, carbon crediting and sustainable cotton markets, and empower the rural agricultural workforce via employment generation.

[normal] During the 2025 reporting period, field experiments were conducted at the Texas A&M Research Farm in Snook, Burleson County, Texas, to evaluate the effects of bed configuration and cover crop termination timing on cotton productivity. Treatments included non-raised bed, raised bed, and raised furrow planting systems, with and without cover crops terminated two and four weeks before planting, arranged in a randomized complete block design. Research activities included cover crop termination, cotton planting, field management, biomass sampling, and manual harvest to determine lint yield. Yield data were collected, and preliminary analyses were completed to compare treatment effects among management systems. Results showed that non-raised bed treatments produced slightly higher lint yields than raised bed systems, although treatment differences were not statistically significant. Cover crop treatments generally maintained or improved yield performance, particularly under the two-week termination treatment. These activities contributed to evaluating regenerative cotton management strategies for improving long-term sustainability in Texas Blackland Vertisols.

[normal] Darren Hudson (Obj-3), Texas Tech University
[normal] We completed a meta-analysis of precision application and soil health agronomic practices. Data on yield, profitability, and environmental outcomes were collected from U.S.-based agronomic research involving guidance systems, variable rate application, cover crops, and pasture management across US crops (primarily corn, wheat, and cotton), along with fruits and vegetables and pastures. Findings indicates for cotton that guidance systems and variable rate planters were the most important for yield and profit improvements. In addition to this, we completed an analysis of the risk and insurance impacts of soil health improvements in cotton. Analysis was based on a Monte Carlo simulation using agronomic data from the Texas High Plains and simulated yield impacts of soil health improvements. Findings indicated that soil health improvements significantly improve yield and reduce the risk of crop failure in dryland cotton systems. However, the increased yield increases insurance costs. However, the added yield benefits outweigh increased insurance costs.
